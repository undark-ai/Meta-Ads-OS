# Ad Formulas by Funnel Stage

Six named formulas for the headline that carries an ad — the line doing the work whether it sits on the image, in the primary text, or as the first spoken sentence of a video. Each has a structure, a funnel stage where it lands hardest, and a way to write one.

Unlike the rhetorical-device techniques in this skill's other references, these are **structural** formulas: they define what the line is *doing* to the reader, not what figure of speech it uses. The two compose freely — a Contrast headline can be built with alliteration.

## The Headline Is the Creative

Before the formulas, the constraint that makes them matter: in a static ad the headline usually **is** the creative. Practitioner-reported distributions from high-performing static ads:

- **Dark or high-contrast backgrounds: ~40%.** Bright saturated colour: ~35%. Light or white: ~15–20%.
- **Headline text occupying 50–70% of the image area.** Not a caption on a photo — the text is the image.
- **One sentence.** If it needs two, it needs two ads.
- **Readable at thumbnail size.** Test it by shrinking it to the size it will actually appear at in a feed.

Include: one sentence, a small corner logo, optionally an embedded CTA, one accent colour. Exclude: bullet lists, stock photography, multi-element layouts, feature lists, small text.

**The D2C correction.** These distributions come from a professional-network feed, where text-as-creative stands out against a wall of headshots and articles. In a shopping context — Instagram feed, Reels, TikTok — **product-hero imagery and UGC-native video frequently beat text-as-creative**, because the buyer is in a browsing mindset and wants to see the thing. Treat text-as-creative as one strong format among several, not the default. It performs best for problem-first and category-challenging angles, and worst for anything where the product's appearance is the selling point.

## The Six Formulas

### 1. Feeling

**Structure:** `[Positive state], not [negative state].`

Names the emotional outcome and the emotional cost of the status quo in one breath. Says nothing about the product.

- "Clear skin, not a 9-step routine."
- "Sleep, not scrolling."
- "Confidence, not a filter."

**Why it works:** at the top of the funnel the reader has a feeling, not a product category. This meets them there and asks nothing.

**Stage:** Top of funnel, cold.

**How to write one:** name the state the customer wants in one or two words. Name the state they're in now. Put them in that order — desired first, current second. Cut every word that isn't one of those two states.

### 2. Conversation

**Structure:** two to four lines of overheard dialogue, ending on the pain. The product is never mentioned.

- *"'Did you sleep?'* / *'I got in bed at 10.'* / *'That's not what I asked.'*"

**Why it works:** dialogue is processed as a scene rather than a claim, so it bypasses the reflex that discounts advertising. The reader recognizes themselves in someone else's exchange.

**Stage:** Top of funnel, cold.

**How to write one:** find the sentence a real customer says about the problem — from reviews, support tickets, post-purchase surveys. Build the two lines that make someone say it. Land the punchline on the pain, not on the product. If a brand name appears anywhere, it isn't this formula.

### 3. Contrast

**Structure:** `No more X. No more Y. Just Z.` · `Less X. More Y.` · `[Before] → [After]`

- "No pills. No powders. Just breakfast."
- "45 minutes → 4 minutes."

**Why it works:** it does the comparison for the reader instead of asking them to hold two states in mind. D2C already lives on before-and-after; this is its verbal form.

**Stage:** Middle of funnel — the reader knows the category and is comparing.

**How to write one:** list what the product removes. List what it adds. Cut to the two most concrete items on each side. Numbers beat adjectives on both sides.

### 4. Category Challenge

**Structure:** frame the status quo as something the reader has been misled about — not something they were stupid to accept.

- "Every 'clean' sunscreen still uses the same three filters. Here's what that means."
- "The reason drugstore retinol burns has nothing to do with your skin."

**Why it works:** it gives the reader a reason to reconsider without asking them to admit a mistake.

**The important reframe.** The B2B version of this formula shames the reader's *process* — "It's 2026, why is your forecast still in a spreadsheet?" — which works when the target is a workflow nobody personally identifies with. **Do not point that at a consumer.** Shaming someone's current shampoo, diet or body is a different act, it converts worse, and in regulated categories it is a compliance problem. Aim the challenge at the **category, the industry, or the marketing** — never at the person.

**Stage:** Middle of funnel.

**How to write one:** find the thing the category does that a well-informed buyer would object to. State it plainly. Then check: does this make the reader feel misled, or does it make them feel stupid? If the second, rewrite it.

### 5. Stat Interrupt

**Structure:** one dramatic number as the largest element on the page, with the barest context.

- "4,000 reviews. 4.8 stars."
- "One wash. 200 loads."
- "12 grams of sugar in a 'healthy' smoothie."

**Why it works:** a specific number is processed as fact before the reader decides whether to trust it. It buys the second of attention that the rest of the ad needs.

**Rules:** the number must be **real and current**. Round numbers read as invented — 4,095 is more credible than "4,000+". Never construct a stat from a claim the brand hasn't substantiated.

**Stage:** Any, and unusually flexible across all of them.

**How to write one:** find the most surprising true number the brand owns — reviews, ingredient counts, wash counts, time saved, a competitor's figure. Make it the largest thing on the page. Add the minimum words needed to make it mean something.

### 6. Pain + Outcome

**Structure:** `[Pain verb] the [old thing], [outcome verb] [result].`

- "Ditch the 9-step routine, wake up with clear skin."
- "Stop guessing your ring size. Get one that fits."

Under 10 words. Strong verbs on both sides — pain: *ditch, cut, stop, drop, skip, quit, end*; outcome: *get, gain, keep, see, wake up with, finally*.

**Why it works:** it is the entire value proposition in one line, which is exactly what a bottom-funnel reader wants and exactly what a cold reader ignores.

**Stage:** Middle to bottom of funnel.

**How to write one:** name the thing they'll stop doing. Name the result they'll get. Pick the strongest verb for each. Cut to under ten words.

## Quick Reference

| Formula | Structure | Stage | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feeling | `[Positive], not [negative]` | Cold | The states are abstract rather than felt |
| Conversation | 2–4 lines of dialogue, ending on the pain | Cold | It reads as written rather than overheard |
| Contrast | `No more X. Just Z.` / before → after | Middle | The two sides aren't concrete |
| Category Challenge | The category misled you | Middle | It shames the person instead of the category |
| Stat Interrupt | One real number, largest element | Any | The number is rounded, stale, or invented |
| Pain + Outcome | `[Pain verb] the [old], [outcome verb] [result]` | Middle–bottom | It's over ten words, or the verbs are weak |

## Writing Sequence

1. **Get the voice-of-customer material first.** Reviews (yours and competitors'), post-purchase surveys, support tickets, comment sections. Pull pain phrases, outcome phrases, specific numbers, emotional words. See **customer-research** for the method and creative-taxonomy for the persona and phrase-library axes — the rule there is that a phrase counts once it recurs across independent sources.
2. **Pick the ONE idea.** One ad, one claim.
3. **Choose the formula** from the stage and the idea.
4. **Write the image text first**, then test it: would this stop a scroll with no body copy at all?
5. **Write the body copy** to earn the click the headline created.
6. **Make the headline field say something different** from the image. Repeating it wastes the only other line you get.
7. **Audit before shipping:** is every factual claim substantiated? Does the tone match the brand? Is it one idea? Is the design dense enough to read at thumbnail size? Does it name a real buyer?
